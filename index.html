Before the first vote

In the remote town of Gloam, ten teenagers are brought to an abandoned school for a game no one remembers agreeing to play. They have the same face, the same build, the same clothes. Outside the voting chamber, even their voices are filtered into the same flat tone. No one can know who stands beside them—not without risking everything.

Seven are innocent. Three are imposters. The rules promise that the truth can be found. The rules do not promise that the truth will be easy to recognize.

The rules as the players understand them

  • Ten players: seven innocents and three imposters.
  • Identical appearances: players cannot identify anyone outside voting.
  • Notes: only the writer can read a note before placing it. Once it touches a surface, it locks in place. Its author is unknown unless witnessed.
  • Identify tool: one use per player for the entire story. It reveals the target's real name above their head for five seconds, then disappears. No mark remains.
  • Position Swap: each imposter has two uses during the middle of the school vote. Each use randomly swaps their position with another player; the imposter must act naturally afterward.
  • Secret selection: an imposter can tap a chosen victim at any time. When voting begins, that victim is gone.
  • Voting: the group votes someone out. Nobody is told to trust a face.
Chapter One

The Town That Had No Road Out

The bus arrived in Gloam at 4:17 in the afternoon, though the sky had already turned the colour of a bruise.

There were no houses along the last stretch of road. No petrol station, no welcome sign, no telephone wires beside the trees. Just dark pines, a narrow road, and wind pushing dead leaves across the asphalt as if the forest were sweeping away its own tracks.

Ten teenagers sat in ten separate seats. Each wore a grey jacket, dark trousers, and plain black shoes. Each had a small metal case on their lap. Each had been told to keep the case closed until the bus stopped.

When the brakes sighed, all ten looked up at once.

Outside stood a school building with three storeys and windows like black mirrors. A faded sign over the entrance read GLOAM SECONDARY. The clock above it had no hands.

The bus driver did not turn around. “You're here.”

One teenager leaned forward. “Here for what?”

The driver opened the doors. “The Torches Wind.”

No one laughed. It sounded less like a game's name than a warning repeated until its meaning had worn away.

They stepped down into the cold. The bus pulled away before the last teenager had reached the pavement. Its red lights vanished around a bend. A moment later, the engine was gone too.

One tried a phone. No service. Another tried the road back. The wind pushed against their chest, hard enough to make them stumble.

Then the school doors opened by themselves.

Inside, the entrance hall smelled of wet stone and overheated electronics. Ten brass hooks lined the wall. Ten identical black faceplates hung from them, each with a small white number on the forehead.

A screen flickered above reception: WELCOME, PLAYERS. PLACE YOUR CASES ON THE TABLE.

The cases opened with a simultaneous click. Inside each was a slim metal tool, a blank notebook, and a pencil. On the inside lid, printed in clean black letters, was one word: IDENTIFY.

Another line appeared: ONE USE. ONE NAME. FIVE SECONDS. NO SECOND CHANCE.

“What does that mean?” someone asked.

The speakers crackled. “You will learn when it matters.”

A second screen lit up. Ten silhouettes stood in a circle around a small flame. Seven were pale. Three were black.

SEVEN INNOCENTS. THREE IMPOSTERS. FIND THEM BEFORE THEY FIND YOU.

There was a long, hollow silence. Then the school doors slammed shut behind them.

Chapter Two

Ten Faces, None of Them Familiar

The faceplates changed the way each person looked. In the dark window, each reflection showed the same stranger—one of ten identical strangers. Same height. Same hair. Same neutral expression. Even the tiny differences that should have given them away had disappeared.

“Say something,” one demanded.

“Something,” answered nine voices at once.

They fell silent.

A soft chime sounded. Screens showed a map of the school: classrooms, gymnasium, library, cafeteria, and a central room labelled VOTING HALL. A timer began counting down from twenty minutes.

PREPARATION PHASE. EXPLORE THE BUILDING. NOTES MAY BE WRITTEN AND PLACED. ONCE PLACED, A NOTE CANNOT BE MOVED.

They split up, though nobody wanted to be alone. The classrooms were abandoned. Every whiteboard had been wiped blank except one sentence in red marker: YOU CANNOT RECOGNIZE WHAT YOU CANNOT SEE.

In the library, a teenager found a sheet of paper tucked inside a book. It was blank until lifted. Words appeared only while the paper was held.

DON'T TRUST THE FIRST PERSON WHO OFFERS TO HELP.

The moment the teenager laid it on the table, the paper stiffened. The message remained visible, but its writer was a mystery.

“Could be anyone,” someone said from the doorway.

The teenager turned. The person there looked exactly like the teenager who had turned.

“That's the point,” came the answer.

In the gym, a screen showed the Identify tool: touch it to a player, and their name appears above their head for five seconds. The target feels no pain and carries no mark. The tool vanishes after use. One use per player. Ten chances to learn ten names—if anyone dared to spend theirs.

At the far end of the gym, someone had scratched two words into a locked cupboard: POSITION SWAP.

A speaker clicked. A tiny flame bloomed on the screen.

THE FIRST VOTE BEGINS IN SIXTY SECONDS.

They returned to the hall. Their faceplates dissolved into the air. For the first time since the bus, their real appearances returned. They stared at one another as if seeing the world again. Then the lights flickered, and the voting doors opened.

Chapter Three

The First Name

The voting hall had ten chairs arranged in a circle and a single black table in the middle. A screen showed each teenager's real face, name, and seat number. For the duration of the vote, identity was visible. It felt like a gift. It felt like a trap.

“We need rules,” said Mara, a girl whose voice shook, though she kept her eyes on the others. “Nobody votes without saying why.”

“We don't know each other,” said Jonas, a broad-shouldered boy with tired eyes. “A reason can be invented.”

“Then we look for contradictions.”

Eli raised his hand. “What if the imposters are the ones making the rules?” Nobody answered him.

The screen displayed the names. Each player received a private voting pad. A vote could be changed until the final ten seconds. After that, it was permanent.

They argued for six minutes. One accused another of walking too quietly. Someone pointed out that every person had walked quietly because the corridors were carpeted. A third accused a teenager of writing the library note. The accused replied they had never been near the library.

“Can anyone prove where they were?” Mara asked.

Ten faces looked back at her.

“No,” said Jonas. “That's the problem.”

At the final chime, everyone pressed a name. The result appeared: SEAT 6 — NOAH.

Noah stood slowly. He looked less angry than confused. “You have no idea what you're doing,” he said.

“Do you?” Mara asked.

He looked at her for a long moment. “I thought I did.”

A door opened behind his chair. White light filled the doorway. Noah walked through it, and the door shut without a sound.

The screen displayed no role. No explanation. Only: ONE PLAYER REMOVED. THE GAME CONTINUES.

They had voted someone out, but learned nothing about whether he was innocent. That uncertainty was worse than a wrong answer. It meant even a correct vote might feel like a mistake.

When the faceplates returned, nine identical figures stood in the hall. One space was empty. And in the corner, where no one remembered seeing it before, a small black torch burned with a flame that gave off no heat.

Chapter Four

A Note That Could Not Be Taken Back

The second preparation phase began with a bell that sounded like a spoon striking a glass. They had learned the first lesson: voting could remove a person without telling them what they had done.

A new note had appeared on the library table. Nobody had seen it being placed. Its message was short: ONE OF YOU IS COUNTING WRONG.

“Counting what?” Eli asked.

“People,” Jonas said.

They counted each other. Nine people. Nine names. No missing chair, no hidden door open, no empty place in the circle.

“The note wants us frightened,” Mara said.

“Or it wants us to check something we've forgotten,” Eli replied.

Eli took out his Identify tool. It was cold in his palm. He could use it on anyone, and for five seconds the name above that person's head would tell him the truth. Then the tool would be gone forever.

Everyone watched him.

“If I use this, I won't get another chance.”

“No one does,” Mara said. “That's what it says.”

“So how do I choose?”

“You don't choose the person you fear most,” Jonas said. “You choose the person whose identity would tell us the most.”

“That sounds like something an imposter would say.”

“Everything sounds like something an imposter would say.”

Eli lowered the tool. He had expected certainty to feel like a light in a dark room. Instead, it felt like a match he was afraid to strike.

He put it away.

That night, Mara wrote a note in her room: IF YOU ARE INNOCENT, DO NOT TRY TO LOOK INNOCENT. TRY TO REMEMBER WHAT YOU ACTUALLY DID. She placed it on the desk. The paper stiffened. It was fixed there now, impossible to move.

When she turned away, a shadow crossed the window.

Mara spun around. There was no one outside. Only the trees, bowing in the wind.

Chapter Five

The Middle of the Vote

The next voting session began with a new rule on the screen: MID-VOTE EVENT: POSITION SWAP. TWO ACTIVATIONS WILL OCCUR. OBSERVE CAREFULLY.

“Two?” Eli whispered.

“Two is enough to ruin everything,” Mara answered.

The nine teenagers sat beneath the voting lights, their real faces visible. The first half of the vote passed in tense arguments. They had learned to ask for details: where someone had been, which hand had held a note, what time a bell had sounded. They had learned that a confident memory could still be wrong.

Then every screen went dark. A low mechanical hum rolled beneath the floor. The lights flashed once. The chairs clicked against the tiles.

When the lights returned, everyone was still sitting—but not everyone was where they had been.

“Nobody move,” Mara said.

“I didn't,” said Jonas.

“I know. I'm saying nobody move now.”

They compared seat numbers. A person who had been in seat two was now in seat eight. Someone else had moved to seat two. The screen insisted the swap was random. It did not say who had activated it, or why.

“If a person changes position, their identity doesn't change,” Eli said. “Remember the people, not the seats.”

“We can't recognize people outside this room,” Jonas replied. “And we can't prove what we remember.”

The vote resumed. A minute later, the hum returned. The second Position Swap activated.

This time, one teenager began to stand before the lights had fully returned, then stopped, as if remembering a rule no one else could see. Another gripped the edge of the table. A third stared at the screen with a blankness that looked rehearsed.

“Who moved?” Mara asked.

Nobody answered. They could compare seats, but a seat was only a place. It was not a name. It was not proof.

Then a chair at the far end of the circle stood empty. Mara was certain someone had been sitting there. She remembered the slight tapping of fingers against the table, a tiny rhythm that had continued through the arguments.

But the screen showed only eight active names.

“Who was there?” Eli asked.

Nobody could answer quickly enough. The display flickered: A PLAYER HAS BEEN REMOVED FROM THE SESSION.

No name. No role. No body. Just a missing place where a person had been a moment before.

Eight players remained. Somewhere in the school, a note was being written by someone whose handwriting no one would ever be able to recognize.

Chapter Six

The Shape of a Lie

By morning, the wind had become so loud that it shook the window frames. The speakers announced the road remained closed. No buses would arrive. No calls would leave the building. The next vote would begin at noon.

Eight teenagers gathered in the cafeteria. Nobody touched the food. Each tray had been set out in advance, each cup filled to the same line. Even the portions looked identical, as though the school had decided that differences were dangerous.

Jonas took a blank note and held it up. “We need a system. When we leave a room, we write where we're going. We place the note where everyone can find it. Nobody moves it. If a note appears without a witness, we treat it as information—not proof.”

“And what if the writer lies?” Eli asked.

“Then we don't trust the note. We check whether the details fit what we know.”

Mara nodded. “A note can tell us what someone wants us to believe. It can't tell us who wrote it.”

They agreed to use a hand signal: two fingers raised meant I am the person you saw a moment ago. The response was a closed fist. It was not perfect. Anyone could copy it. But it gave them something to test, a pattern that could fail.

They tried it in pairs. Two fingers. Closed fist. Two fingers. Closed fist.

Then, from the corridor, came three sharp knocks. Every teenager turned.

A note lay on the cafeteria threshold. None had seen it arrive. Mara picked it up. The words appeared while she held it: THE SIGNAL IS USELESS IF YOU DON'T KNOW WHO TAUGHT IT TO YOU.

She set the note on the floor. It locked in place.

“That's true,” Eli said. “But that doesn't make the signal useless. It means we can't use it as the only proof.”

“Nothing is proof,” Jonas said.

“No. Some things are proof. We just haven't found them yet.”

The school bell rang. Their faceplates returned. The cafeteria lights dimmed, and the voting hall doors opened at the end of the corridor.

Mara walked toward them with the others. She kept her hands visible. She tried to remember every step, every sound, every position in the room. She would not trust a feeling simply because it was strong.

At the entrance, she noticed a torch symbol scratched into the paint. Its flame pointed toward the floor, but the wind-shaped lines around it curled upward. Below it, someone had carved a sentence so lightly it could only be read from the side: The first person you lose is not always the first person who disappears.

Mara read it twice. Then she stepped into the hall. Eight chairs waited under the lights. Eight names appeared on the screen. And the next vote began.

Chapter Seven

What the Wind Carried

The third vote was different because nobody wanted to be the first to speak.

They had begun with accusations, then moved to theories, then to systems. Now every system had a weakness. A note could be planted. A memory could be mistaken. A seat could change. A signal could be copied. An Identify tool could reveal a name, but it could not explain what that person had done.

“We should use the tools,” someone said.

“On whom?” Mara asked.

“On whoever seems suspicious.”

“Everyone seems suspicious.”

The timer ticked down. One teenager claimed to have seen someone near the library after the second swap. Another insisted the library had been empty. A third remembered footsteps in the gym, but could not remember whether they had been approaching or moving away.

Jonas stood. “We keep treating this as a puzzle with one clever answer. It might be a puzzle with ten incomplete answers. We have to compare them.”

He asked each person to describe the same ten-minute stretch of the previous evening. Not what they believed had happened. Only what they remembered seeing, hearing, or doing.

The first account was precise. The second contradicted it. The third contradicted both, but contained one small detail that matched the note in the library: a clock that had chimed when no clock was supposed to work.

“Which one of you heard the chime?” Mara asked.

Four hands rose.

“That's impossible,” Eli said. “The sound came from the east corridor. We were in different places.”

“Or we were not where we thought we were,” Jonas said.

The thought settled among them like dust. They checked the map. The east corridor was beside the old music room, which had no clock. But the room above it had a wall clock, and the floor plan showed a narrow maintenance staircase connecting the two corridors.

“Someone could have moved between floors,” Mara said.

“Anyone could have,” Eli replied. “That doesn't identify anyone.”

“No,” she said. “But it tells us what to ask next.”

The timer reached ten seconds. They voted. A name appeared on the screen. The person stood, shoulders squared, and walked to the white doorway. Before stepping through, they turned back.

“You think the game wants you to find the imposters,” they said. “Maybe it wants you to find out what you'll do when you can't.”

The door closed. The screen showed no role. Seven players remained in the circle, but the number was not the only thing that had changed. The rules had given them a way to remove people. They had not given them a way to know whether they were right.

Outside, the wind pressed hard against the school. For a moment, the old building seemed to breathe. Then a speaker clicked on: THE NEXT PHASE WILL BEGIN AFTER DARK.

Every light in the hall went out except the small flame on the black torch. Its shadow pointed toward the exit.

For the first time, Mara wondered whether the town was keeping them in—or keeping something else out.

End of Part One

The game has begun, but the truth remains buried beneath identical faces, locked notes, and memories that may not be enough. The identities of the imposters and the full explanation of the Torches Wind remain unrevealed.
